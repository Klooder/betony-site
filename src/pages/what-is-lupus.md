# What is lupus?

Systemic lupus erythematosus, usually called lupus, is a chronic autoimmune disease: the immune system attacks the body's own tissues. This causes inflammation that can affect the skin, joints, heart, lungs, kidneys, blood cells and brain. It is not contagious.

## Symptoms
Symptoms vary from person to person and range from mild to severe. They can come and go, and new ones can develop over time. They include:
- painful, swollen joints and morning stiffness
- extreme tiredness that does not go away with rest
- a rash across the nose and cheeks, called a butterfly or malar rash
- sensitivity to the sun
- fevers
- hair loss
- mouth and nose sores
- fingers and toes changing colour in the cold or under stress (Raynaud's)
- pain when breathing deeply
- headaches

## Flares and remission
People with lupus often have periods of illness, called flares, and periods of wellness, called remission. The NIH describes flares as unpredictable and ranging from mild to serious. The NHS notes that the reason symptoms flare up or settle down is not known, and that some people's symptoms stay constant.

## Who gets lupus
Anyone can get lupus, but women get it about nine times more often than men. It most often begins between the ages of 15 and 45. It is more common in African American people and in people of American Indian and Asian descent, and having a family member with lupus or another autoimmune disease raises the likelihood.

## Diagnosis and treatment
Lupus can be hard to diagnose because it resembles other conditions. A diagnosis usually rests on blood tests showing certain antibodies together with typical symptoms. There is no cure, but the NIH says that with treatment many people can manage the disease. Treatment is chosen with a doctor, and people with lupus are usually advised to have regular checks.
